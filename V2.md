# The Software Engineering Manifesto

The Manifesto for Agile Software Development has guided our industry for over twenty-five years, and its impact cannot be overstated.

But the assumptions it was built on no longer hold. The nature of engineering has shifted, and the pace of how we deliver software has accelerated beyond what that original document envisioned. AI and the tools available today have fundamentally changed what individuals and small teams can achieve — and what the work of engineering itself looks like.

We are already operating in a new era of software engineering, defined by how software is produced, validated, and understood.

Engineering remains the act of turning what the business needs into software that runs. What has changed is everything required to do that well. Engineers no longer primarily write code; they direct its creation and validate its outcomes. Code can now be produced with minimal effort; more of the work now lies in understanding the problem, making good decisions about what to build, and ensuring what ships actually delivers value.

The standard for understanding what we build, why we build it, and what it becomes hasn't moved; if anything, it has gone up.

Built on the same foundation, this is a manifesto for how software engineering works now.

---

## Values

We are uncovering better ways of engineering software by building it and helping others build it.

Through this work we have come to value:

**Engineering turns what the business needs into software that runs.** Anything that doesn't connect those two ends isn't engineering.

**Engineering starts with understanding the customer and the problem** — not the brief, not the ticket, but what they actually need.

**Software is built by capable people, accelerated by tools.** Judgement, care, and sustainability come from the engineers — tools compound what's already there.

**Feedback loops should exist everywhere** — shorten and amplify them, they are how you find out you're wrong before it's expensive, and right before it's too late.

**What you ship, you own** — ownership means caring about how fast it goes out, how well it lands, and how securely and reliably it runs.

---

## Principles

We follow these principles:

1. Our highest priority is a working product that delivers measurable value to the customer.

2. Continuous attention to technical excellence and sound architecture are critical. Weak standards are magnified by faster delivery.

3. Deep understanding of the problem precedes every good solution. Working software — prototype or production — is how you best learn what is actually needed. Speed without understanding produces output, not engineering.

4. Build around motivated, capable individuals; equip them with tools that compound their judgement and amplify their capability. Trust them to get the job done.

5. Roles converge around the customer problem — everyone shaping a product should be deep in their discipline and capable across the others.

6. Clear communication and strong, constant collaboration are how engineering scales beyond individuals. Misalignment between people significantly slows delivery.

7. Welcome change driven by learned evidence. Resist change driven by anything else. When evidence disagrees with the plan, the plan loses.

8. At regular intervals, the team reflects on how to become more effective, then commits to actions and follows through. Every reflection should show the team measurably better and continuously improving.

9. Deliver and deploy continuously in small increments, but manage the rate of change that customers experience.

10. Engineers are ultimately accountable for what they ship. Accountability for the software cannot be delegated to the tools used to build it.

11. Continuous automated verification and deliberate human review are as important as the code they validate.

12. Documentation is operational infrastructure, not overhead — it should live alongside the code it describes, and be readable by both the humans and the tools that work on it.

13. Systems built on bad data produce bad outcomes and are only as good as the data they learn from, retrieve from, and act on.

14. Simplicity — the art of maximising the amount of work not done — is essential. Remove unessential complexity from requirements, from the software, and from the team processes.

15. Work should be done at a sustainable pace that protects both the people and their ability to deliver, indefinitely.
